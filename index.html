A loss-leader first job pays for itself in about six hours

The standard advice says take the cheap first job, collect the review, move on. Almost nobody prices the move. A first project is an ad campaign with a budget, and the budget is the gap between your normal rate and what you charged. Priced on purpose, it is cheap advertising. Priced by accident, it is a raise you handed a stranger.

Suppose your work clears $35 an hour everywhere else. A first client offers $500 flat for about twenty hours, which lands the effective rate at $25 an hour. The gap is the entire campaign budget: (35 - 25) x 20 = $200. That $200 is what the first job cost you, and it belongs on the same mental line as an ad bill.

The payback question is an hours count, not a feeling. At your full rate, $200 / $35 = 5.7 hours of repeat work buys the discount back. Call it six. A client who books six more hours has made the loss-leader free, and every hour after that out-earns the advertising.

Run it on a real shape. The client returns with a project worth 30 x $35 = $1,050. The relationship now reads $1,550 across fifty hours, a blended $31 an hour, which sits 11.4 percent under your rate. That blend is the true cost of buying the review and the relationship in one move, and it is a fair price only where repeat work actually exists.

Where none exists, the discount never comes home. A one-shot gig at $500 for twenty hours is a $10-an-hour raise for the client and $200 gone for good. A retainer shape behaves differently: bookkeeping at 5 hours a month is 60 hours x $35 = $2,100 a year at full rate, the discount across year one is $600, and a client who renews hands back 17 hours in year two at zero discount.
